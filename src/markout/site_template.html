Markout

A reality check for trading backtests.

A backtest tells you what a strategy would have made if every order filled at the price on the screen. Markout measures how much of that survives the spread, a real order queue, other traders and the bias of trying many ideas, on real NASDAQ and S&P 500 data. Every lab on this page runs in your browser.

Lab 1 · simulation

Will your bid get filled?

Join the best bid behind the queue and watch two backtests disagree. A touch-fill backtest counts your order as filled the moment anyone trades at your price. A real queue fills you only after everyone ahead of you has traded or cancelled. Some sellers are informed: when one arrives, the whole level breaks a few seconds later and the price falls.

    Lab 2 · real NASDAQ data

    Does the signal survive real fills?

    Queue imbalance, the gap between the shares waiting at the best bid and at the best ask, predicts the next price move. These results come from replaying every order-book message for five NASDAQ stocks on 21 June 2012 against hypothetical 100-share orders, under three fill models. Pick a stock.

    What happens after a resting order fills

    Can a trader make money with it?

    Every 5 seconds the trader follows the sign of the imbalance and either posts at the best quote, crosses the spread or waits, with the choice made on the morning and scored on the afternoon. Always posting needs no choice at all.

    Lab 5 · game

    Make a market on five hidden cards

    Five cards are dealt face down (A = 1 to K = 13). Quote a bid and an ask on their sum, at most four apart. Three bots trade against you each round: some know one hidden card and only trade when your quote is wrong, the rest trade at random. One card is turned up after every round, and every trade settles at the final sum. At the end the game replays the same cards and bots with two scripted quoters, so you can compare.

    SIG's student prediction-market contest allows bots, so I built one that runs on a laptop.     About this project

    What it does

    Markout asks one question at every stage of a trading idea: how much of the edge is real? It covers a machine-learning forecast of the NASDAQ closing auction, an order-by-order replay of NASDAQ queues, game-theory models of competing market makers, options hedging, the S&P 500 volatility premium, and a trading desk for SIG's Predictions Cup.

    How I built it

    The research is in Python, with walk-forward validation, a log of every variant tried, deflated Sharpe ratios and a holdout that can only be opened once. The order-book replay is written in C++17 with pybind11 bindings and is bit-identical to the Python reference. This site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript; the browser versions of the simulations are tested against the Python code. The desk also runs as a hosted service in Docker, with a private paper-trading sandbox for every visitor, and CI checks types, coverage, a real browser and the container on every push.

    Challenges

    Getting fills right. An early pessimistic fill model filled more orders than the realistic one, which forced a rewrite and a test that the three models nest order by order. Keeping evaluation honest was the other: because every variant is logged, the final numbers account for the whole search.

    What I learned

    A prediction and a profit are different things. Queue imbalance predicts the next price move, but the fills a real queue gives you are the ones you didn't want. And sizing a fat-tailed bet needs the actual distribution of outcomes, not just its mean and variance.

    What's next

    More trading days and recent data, modelling latency and market impact, and connecting the desk to the contest's live API.
